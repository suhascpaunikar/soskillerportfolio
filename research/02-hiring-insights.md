## What reviewers are looking for (consistent across 2026 sources)

1. **Judgment over screens.** Decisions, trade-offs and constraints, not a gallery. *"Hiring managers do not hire glitter. They hire evidence that you can deliver."*
2. **Impact up front.** The first case study has to show business impact in the first scroll.
3. **Ownership.** Exactly which decisions were yours.
4. **Shipped, live, traceable work.** Is it live? Is it scaling? (Yours is: "live in every Lenskart store in India".)
5. **Fewer, deeper projects.** 3–5 case studies, no more. *"Your portfolio is only as strong as your weakest project."*
6. **AI fluency, shown, not claimed.** Which tools, where they fit, what they saved. *"AI signals are not optional"* (Fountain Institute).
7. **Builders.** Designers who ship working prototypes and small tools instead of static write-ups ("the portfolio is becoming a playground"). In India, 2026 AI-product roles (Freshworks, DevRev, Hevo, Sarvam, Karbon and others) explicitly ask for end-to-end ownership and AI-feature work.
8. **Specialists.** Companies are prioritising domain expertise plus measurable business impact. For you: **commerce, checkout, retail/omnichannel, in-store tools, AI-assisted workflows.**

## How to present the AI project (it's your most "2026" asset)

Research on presenting AI work points to:
- **Trust calibration, not trust maximisation.** Aim for *appropriate reliance*: users know when to rely, when to question, when to step in.
- **Design *for* uncertainty instead of hiding it** (Atlassian's lesson).
- **Human-in-the-loop as part of the job**, not a safety layer bolted on.
- Document the **confidence, provenance, correction, failure and override** states.

Your project already does all of this (85% parity → manual stays source of truth → paired captures become training data). Name it explicitly; it's a differentiator.

## Design rules that fall out of this research

These are **non-negotiable** whichever concept you pick:

1. **The 10-second test.** Without scrolling or clicking, a visitor must get: *who you are · what you specialise in · one or two proof numbers · how to contact or hire you.*
2. **Concept serves comprehension.** The fun idea must make the content *faster* to understand, never slower. If a reviewer has to learn a UI before seeing your work, the idea failed.
3. **Two depths per case study.** A **30-second version** (outcome, role, the one key decision, metrics) plus the **full story** behind it.
4. **Show the messy middle.** Keep V1's failure and the user calls. That's where trust is built.
5. **Playable proof.** At least one interactive moment per flagship case study, recreating the actual mechanic you designed.
6. **Mobile is the first look.** Recruiters often open links from LinkedIn on a phone and deep-review on desktop later (anecdotal, but consistent). The phone version has to pass the 10-second test too.
7. **Readable by machines.** Semantic HTML, real text (not text baked into images or canvas), a meta description, OG images, and optionally an `llms.txt`. Product designer Brian Lovin already publishes one: https://brianlovin.com/llms.txt
8. **No obvious AI slop.** No AI-generated hero images, no generic AI copy. 43% call AI overuse a red flag, and 2026's visual culture is actively rewarding human, specific, imperfect work (see `03-design-trends-2026.md`).
